# Early childhood practice

Pre-K is not grade zero. Material for three- to five-year-olds fails most often by being a smaller
version of kindergarten work — the same task, fewer items, larger print — which is a different
mistake from being too hard.

## There is no Common Core below kindergarten

This is the first thing to establish, because the alignment claim printed on most pre-K material is
either wrong or vague.

What does apply:

- **State early learning standards**, which exist in every state and vary substantially in structure
  and in what they expect at each age.
- **The Head Start Early Learning Outcomes Framework**, which covers birth to five across
  approaches to learning, social-emotional, language and literacy, cognition, and perceptual and
  physical development. It is a federal work and freely usable.
- **NAEYC's position on developmentally appropriate practice**, which is not a standards document at
  all — it is a statement of what practice should look like, and is the thing most often cited when
  a task is challenged.

A claim of "Common Core aligned" on pre-K material is not defensible.
`education:standards-alignment` covers alignment above kindergarten.

## Developmentally appropriate is a range, not a line

The useful question is not whether a four-year-old can do a task. Some can. It is whether a typical
four-year-old can do it without the experience becoming one of failing.

Ranges worth holding, with the caveat that variation at these ages is wide and normal:

- **Fine motor.** Tripod grasp is still consolidating at four. Tracing within narrow lines,
  small-square handwriting and precision cutting are ahead of most of them, and repeated failure at
  a motor task teaches avoidance of the subject it was attached to.
- **Attention.** Sustained focus on an adult-directed task runs in single-digit minutes. A page that
  takes twenty minutes is not one task, it is four, and it should be designed as four.
- **Counting and cardinality.** Reciting number names comes well before understanding that the last
  number said is how many there are. Material that assumes the second because the first is fluent
  misreads the child.
- **Letters.** Recognition, naming, sound and formation are four separate skills that develop at
  different rates. Name-writing typically precedes general letter formation, because the motivation
  is different.
- **Symbolic reasoning.** A picture standing for a thing is not automatic at three and is usually
  secure by five. Worksheets depend on it entirely.

## Play is the method, which constrains the format

The consensus position across the early childhood field is that young children learn through play,
exploration and interaction — not that play is a reward after the work.

This is uncomfortable for anyone producing worksheets, and the honest conclusion is that **the
worksheet is a supporting format at this age, not the primary one**. Material that acknowledges this
is better material: a page that prompts an activity, a conversation or a hunt around the room does
more than the same page asking for tracing, and it is what a knowledgeable reviewer looks for.

Where a page is the deliverable, the ones that work at this age tend to be open rather than
closed — draw, sort, match, find — with no single correct answer to get wrong.

## Pushdown is the failure mode to name

Academic expectations have migrated downward, so kindergarten now carries work that was first grade
a generation ago, and pre-K material follows. The pressure is commercial: parents buy what looks
rigorous, and "gets them ready" sells.

Being explicit about this is part of doing the work honestly. A pre-K page asking for sentence
writing is not ambitious, it is misaimed, and the child who cannot do it concludes something about
themselves rather than about the page. When a buyer asks for material a year ahead, that is a real
request and it deserves a straight answer about what it will cost the child.

## Never

- Claim Common Core alignment for material below kindergarten.
- Treat a pre-K task as a kindergarten task with fewer items.
- Require fine-motor precision the age has not typically reached.
- Design a single page that needs more sustained attention than the age sustains.
- Present a worksheet as the primary way a three-year-old should be learning.
